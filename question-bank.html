Week 1 — Prologue, Appendix
Instructor's own Question Bank — Prof. Choi says over 50% of Test A/B comes directly from this list
01Why is it important to learn history when studying acupuncture?
History gives you the context that makes the medicine coherent rather than a list of memorized techniques. The book frames this directly: understanding AHM's identity depends on knowing where its ideas came from, the same way understanding Shakespeare requires knowing British history and culture. Without that context, a practitioner can perform techniques without understanding why they work the way they do, or how the tradition's logic holds together.
✓ verified against course material
02What is the difference between TCM history and Medical history?
"Medical history" is the broad, general history of medicine as a human activity across all cultures. "TCM history" is specific: it's the history of one particular tradition, inseparable from Chinese philosophy, cosmology, dynastic politics, and cultural identity — and "TCM" itself is a specific, modern (20th-century, 1959-standardized) institutional construction, not a synonym for "Chinese medicine" across all of history. So TCM history is really a subset — the recent, standardized chapter — within the much longer and more diverse history of Chinese and East Asian medicine.
✓ verified against course material

Week 2 — Chapter 1 — History of AHM, Dynasties
Instructor's own Question Bank — Prof. Choi says over 50% of Test A/B comes directly from this list
01When the shift from 'Mythos to Logos' occurred around the Yellow River region, it influenced the formation of TCM. (Discuss/confirm)
True, and it's the central move of Chapter 1: the Yellow River region is the traditional cultural cradle where legendary, myth-based accounts of medicine (Pan Gu, the Three Sovereigns) gave way to systematized, reasoned medical theory. That regional cultural cradle is also where the Han-dynasty compilations (Nei Jing, Shang Han Lun) that formalized AHM's theoretical foundation were produced.
✓ verified against course material
02What is the title of the encyclopedia-like book published by Li during the Ming dynasty, which contains comprehensive knowledge about Materia Medica?
Ben Cao Gang Mu (本草綱目), Compendium of Materia Medica, by Li Shi Zhen. Took 27 years to compile; catalogued 1,892 medicinals.
✓ verified against course material
03Discuss the influence of the Mongolian invasion on TCM reform.
The Mongol conquest and establishment of the Yuan dynasty disrupted the existing Song/Jin medical establishment and social order. That disruption is part of the backdrop for the Jin-Yuan "Medical Reform" period and the rise of the Four Great Masters, who broke from prior consensus and developed competing new theoretical schools partly in response to the instability and changing clinical realities of the era.
⚠ double-check against the chapter/recording
04Which dynasty came after the Tang and before the Jin/Yuan dynasty?
Song Dynasty.
✓ verified against course material
05Describe key events that happened during the Ming Dynasty outside of China's borders.
The Ming dynasty runs roughly parallel to the European Renaissance and the Age of Exploration — a period of major cultural, artistic, and scientific transformation in Europe, and of European powers beginning transoceanic exploration and colonization. The book explicitly uses this parallel to help place Ming-dynasty China on a global timeline.
✓ verified against course material
06Did the Chinese civilization start first around the Yangtze River in the south or the Yellow River in the north?
The Yellow River, in the north — the traditionally taught cradle of Chinese civilization (Longshan culture and the earliest dynastic states are centered there), though Yangtze River-region cultures were also significant and are increasingly recognized by archaeologists.
✓ verified against course material
07List the following dynasties in the correct chronological order: Song, Yuan, Qing, Sui, Ming, Han, Tang
Han → Sui → Tang → Song → Yuan → Ming → Qing — exactly the order in the book's own mnemonic, "Happy Sloths Travel Slowly, Yearning Meditative Quietness."
✓ verified against course material
08The "Hundred Schools of Thought" correspond to which period in history?
The Spring and Autumn Period and the Warring States Period.
✓ verified against course material
09Select four options corresponding to the Spring and Autumn Period and the Warring States Period in China. (A. The Romance of Three Kingdoms B. Not united Chinese nations C. Mongolian Invasion D. Aristotle and Plato E. Steel age in China F. Zhuangzi)
B, D, E, F. Political fragmentation among competing states (B), the roughly contemporaneous rise of Greek philosophy under Aristotle and Plato (D, an "In the World" parallel), the transition to iron/steel weaponry (E), and the Daoist philosopher Zhuangzi, active during the Warring States (F). The Romance of Three Kingdoms (A) is Han-Jin transition, centuries later; the Mongolian Invasion (C) is the Yuan dynasty, later still — both are the deliberate wrong-era distractors.
✓ verified against course material
10The following event happened in _______ dynasty: Rapid progress in clinical medicine with significant advancements in diagnostic techniques. Notable progress in standardization of basic theory and acupuncture (Ling Shu). Foundation of Chinese Pharmacy and the Science of Prescriptions.
Han Dynasty — this matches the book's own "Key Characteristics" summary for the Han era: formation of AHM's theoretical foundation (Huang Di Nei Jing), Shang Han Lun, and Shen Nong Ben Cao Jing all trace to this period.
✓ verified against course material

Weeks 3–4 — Chapter 2 — Philosophical Background
Instructor's own Question Bank — Prof. Choi says over 50% of Test A/B comes directly from this list
🐾
Straight from Week 4 class — not in the printed question bankChoi confirms it himself: "Sloth is mascot of our class." He tests yin-yang's relativity with paired examples — "Tuna vs. Sloth — which is yang?" and "Joker vs. Batman — which is yang?" — where the answer flips depending on the comparison axis. He's explicit that yin-yang is not binary: "There is infinite number in between, and that is called four constitution, eight diagram, and 64 diagram" — a direct bridge to Sasang Constitutional Medicine and the I Ching's 64 hexagrams. He also names Xiao Yang / Tai Yang / Xiao Yin / Tai Yin here — vocabulary that resurfaces as the Six Stages in the Shang Han Lun. If Choi asks a yin-yang question on the exam, this is the "discussed in class" answer he's listening for.
01Discuss the philosophical backgrounds of TCM.
Three main schools shaped the theoretical scaffolding: Daoism (the Dao as natural order, wu wei/non-forcing action, yin-yang as the body's self-regulating balance), Confucianism (hierarchy and duty — reflected in organ theory's "ruler/minister" model, and filial-piety-driven attention to elder care, gynecology, and pediatrics), and Legalism (codified, centralized control — the faintest influence, but arguably visible later in standardized formulas and protocols). CO2 asks you to be able to compare and contrast all three directly.
✓ verified against course material
02Based on our class discussions, who created the universe according to Pangu's narrative?
Pangu himself. In the myth, the universe begins as a cosmic egg containing chaos; Pangu splits it into yin (the earth) and yang (the sky), holding them apart as he grows for 18,000 years. After his death, his body's parts become the features of the natural world.
✓ verified against course material
03What is the meaning of "Yang Sheng," and how does it relate to the art of nourishing life in TCM?
Yang Sheng (養生) means "nourishing life" — a preventive, lifestyle-based practice of cultivating health and longevity through diet, moderation, and living in accordance with natural rhythms, rather than treating illness after the fact. It's Daoist in origin and is treated as foundational: prevention and daily self-cultivation come first, with acupuncture and herbal treatment as what you turn to next, not the starting point.
✓ verified against course material
04Which of the following is NOT a foundational concept of early TCM? (Taoism, Buddhism, Confucianism, Capitalism)
Capitalism is the obvious non-answer, but don't stop there — pay attention to the trap: Buddhism is also not a foundational influence on early TCM. Buddhism was imported into China well after Daoism and Confucianism had already shaped early Chinese medical theory; it arrived later via India, not alongside the others. If the question is asking specifically about the earliest philosophical foundations, Buddhism is the more interesting correct answer than Capitalism.
✓ verified against course material
05Is it possible to have both Yin and Yang symptoms simultaneously?
Yes. Yin-yang theory itself explains this as normal, not contradictory — patterns like true heat masked by false cold (or the reverse), or upper-body heat with lower-body cold, are standard descriptions in the tradition. Yin and yang are relative and mutually transforming, not fixed, mutually exclusive states.
✓ verified against course material
06What does "Wu Wei" mean?
Non-forcing, effortless action — acting in alignment with the natural order rather than against it. It's a core Daoist principle: not passivity, but not forcing outcomes either.
✓ verified against course material
07Explain why ancient Chinese people thought mankind was derived from fleas in Pangu's myth.
In some versions of the Pangu myth, after his death the small creatures (fleas/parasites) living on his body become human beings, while larger features of his body become mountains, rivers, and sky. Making humanity a minor, almost incidental byproduct of the cosmic body — rather than its central purpose — reflects a certain humility about humanity's place in the universe, in contrast to creation narratives that put humans at the center of the story.
✓ verified against course material
08Explain the meaning of 間. Explain why this specific character is used for concepts like human, space, and time in ancient Chinese thought.
間 (jiān) means "space between" or "interval." It shows up in 人間 (renjian, "among people" = human society/the human world), 空間 (kongjian, space), and 時間 (shijian, time) because ancient Chinese thought conceived of these concepts relationally — as intervals or relationships between things — rather than as independent, absolute entities existing on their own.
✓ verified against course material

Week 5 — Chapter 3 — Cornerstones of AHM (up to acupuncture)

01Do you think TCM is evolving like other natural sciences? Explain your reasoning.
Open discussion question — no single correct answer, but build your case on evidence from the course: texts were revised over centuries (Nei Jing compiled over generations, Wen Bing theory emerging a thousand years after Shang Han theory), channel theory itself changed shape (see below), and standardization efforts (1959 TCM curriculum) reorganized the field. The counter-argument: TCM's changes weren't always cumulative or falsifiable the way natural science is expected to be — schools sometimes diverged rather than converged on consensus.
⚠ double-check against the chapter/recording
02If TCM evolves like other natural sciences, do you think modern acupuncturists are superior to ancient acupuncturists? Why or why not?
"Superior" is the wrong frame — ancient and modern practitioners worked with different clinical realities, tools, and patient populations, so direct ranking is a category error. What's more defensible is that modern practitioners have access to more accumulated clinical data and cross-checking against biomedicine, while ancient practitioners had direct, unmediated clinical experience the texts were built from.
⚠ double-check against the chapter/recording
03Have channel pathway(s) changed through TCM history? Yes or no?
Yes. Early excavated texts (the Mawangdui-era manuscripts) describe roughly eleven meridians, not the twelve used today, with only two or three connected to internal organs and no acupoints named yet — just channel lines. The now-standard twelve-channel, organ-linked system was assembled gradually, not handed down complete from the start.
✓ verified against course material
04How many formulas were recorded in the government-published 'Taiping Shenghui Fang' during the Song dynasty?
Roughly 17,800+ formulas (commonly cited around 17,880) — the book's own material traces a Song-dynasty escalation in recorded formulas: from about 2,800 in the Zheng Lei Ben Cao to about 5,000 in the Qian Jin Yao Fang lineage, up to this much larger number in the Taiping Shenghui Fang. Double-check the exact figure against the Chapter 3 text directly.
⚠ double-check against the chapter/recording
05How many acupuncture points were introduced in 'Zi Sheng Jing' (Ji Sheng Jing) during the Song dynasty?
359. Confirmed directly in the Week 5 lecture: Choi walks the full point-count evolution — Nei Jing's 365 (symbolic, matching days of the year) → ABC of Acupuncture's 349 → Qing Dynasty texts settling around 361 (still not universal even then) → Song Dynasty's Ji Sheng Jing at 359. His point: none of these numbers is "the" correct one — track the trend of gradual, non-fixed growth, not a single figure.
✓ verified against Week 5 lecture
06How many herbs were added to 'Shen Nong Ben Cao Jing' to create 'Ben Cao Gang Mu' in the Ming dynasty?
About 1,527 more. Shen Nong Ben Cao Jing catalogued 365 medicinals; Li Shi Zhen's Ben Cao Gang Mu expanded that to 1,892 medicinals (1,892 − 365 = 1,527).
✓ verified against course material
07Describe how Shennong discovered new herbs and the methodology behind his work.
According to legend, Shennong ("the Divine Farmer") personally tasted hundreds of plants to test their medicinal and toxic properties, reportedly poisoning himself many times over in the process. Choi's own exam full-credit phrase, verbatim from Week 5: "by testing and testing." He's explicit this is the whole point of the myth — a god who's supposed to already know everything about herbs instead sacrifices himself repeatedly, because the story is a compressed folk-memory of thousands of real ancient doctors running trial-and-error verification across generations, keeping whatever worked. He calls Chinese medical history "just a series of clinical trials" in disguise.
✓ verified against Week 5 lecture — exact full-credit phrase
08How many channels are explained in Mawangdui textbooks?
Roughly eleven, not the twelve used in the standard system today.
✓ verified against course material
08bWhat is the "live point" / "true point" concept in Japanese Toyohari (meridian therapy), and why does Choi say the modern "traditional anatomical chart" is a myth?
Toyohari practitioners locate the live point (活穴) by touch, not measurement, using a two-hand technique: Oshide (押手, the holding/finding hand — the more important of the two) and Sashite (刺手, the inserting hand). Points reveal themselves as tenderness, nodules, or rubbery/tight tissue where chi and blood are obstructed — they shift with disease stage, time of day, and season rather than sitting at a fixed coordinate. Choi traces this concept back 1,500 years to Sun Simiao's Tang-dynasty Qian Jin Yao Fang. His pointed claim: "there is no such a thing" as a traditional anatomical chart — standardized point charts are a 20th-century Chinese invention built for modernization, not something that existed in classical practice.
✓ verified against Week 5 lecture
09Is Aloe vera considered a TCM herb? Explain why or why not.
Yes — Aloe vera (Lu Hui, 蘆薈) appears in the Chinese materia medica, classified as bitter and cold, used for purging heat and treating constipation, despite originating outside China (Africa/Arabian peninsula). This is a good example of the same identity question that comes up with Sun Si Miao's Persian herbs (Week 6): TCM's herbal canon absorbed non-Chinese substances through the Silk Road and other trade routes rather than staying botanically "pure."
✓ verified against course material
10Who is the cultural hero among the Three Sovereigns that introduced Chinese medicine and agriculture?
Shennong (神農).
✓ verified against course material
11What is the name of the first Chinese book on agriculture and medicinal plants?
Shen Nong Ben Cao Jing (神農本草經) is the standard answer in this context — the foundational materia medica attributed to Shennong.
⚠ double-check against the chapter/recording

01Sun Simiao used many herbs from Persia through the Silk Road. Should we also study European or Indian herbs in the framework of Chinese herbs? Explain why we 'should' or 'don't have to'.
Strongest "should" argument: the tradition's own history models integration, not purity — Sun Si Miao himself incorporated foreign substances, so treating the herbal canon as a closed, purely Chinese-origin system misreads its actual history. The "don't have to" counter: clinical training has to bound itself somewhere, and TCM theory (properties, channel entry, combinations) is what defines a "TCM herb," not geographic origin — so the framework matters more than the herb's birthplace.
⚠ double-check against the chapter/recording
02When was Huang Di Nei Jing written?
Compiled around 100 BCE, during the Han dynasty — though it's a composite work assembled and edited over generations, not written at one sitting by one author.
✓ verified against course material
03In your opinion, which religion or philosophy influenced the 'Huang Di Nei Jing' the most?
The defensible answer is Daoism — its cosmology (yin-yang, the Dao as natural order, qi) is the most directly embedded framework running through the text's theory.
⚠ double-check against the chapter/recording
04Who wrote this ethical code during the Tang dynasty: "As a physician, you may not depend on your own greatness [alone]...but you must create a heart intent on relieving suffering"?
Sun Si Miao, from the ethics section of his Bei Ji Qian Jin Yao Fang — the first Chinese medical ethics text.
✓ verified against course material
05When (dynasty) did medical reform based on neo-Confucianism occur in Chinese medical history?
The Jin-Yuan period (the "Medical Reform" era in the course schedule) — associated with the Four Great Masters and a Neo-Confucian intellectual revival that reshaped medical theory.
✓ verified against course material
06What is the title of the first specialized book about pulse diagnosis?
Mai Jing (脈經), by Wang Shu He.
✓ verified against course material
07Who wrote Huang Di Nei Jing?
No single author — traditionally attributed to the legendary Yellow Emperor (Huang Di) in dialogue with his minister Qi Bo, but actually compiled and edited by unknown scholars over generations during the Han dynasty. The attribution to Huang Di functions as a legendary framing device, not a historical authorship claim.
✓ verified against course material
08Who wrote 'Pi Wei Lun'?
Li Dongyuan (Li Gao), one of the Jin-Yuan Four Great Masters, associated with the Spleen-Stomach school.
✓ verified against course material
09Discuss whether Shang Han Lun is a book based on fundamental theories or clinical experience.
Primarily clinical experience. Zhang Zhong Jing wrote it after an epidemic killed roughly two-thirds of his own clan — it's organized around observed clinical stages and concrete treatment protocols rather than abstract cosmological theorizing, even though it draws on Nei Jing theoretical foundations where useful.
✓ verified against course material
10Which school focused more on internal etiology, like the balance of organs, than external pathogens in Yuan dynasty?
Li Dongyuan's Spleen-Stomach school (Internal Damage theory) — in contrast to masters who emphasized external pathogenic factors like heat/fire.
✓ verified against course material
11What is the incorrect description about AHM/TCM diagnosis? (A. tongue diagnosis is the only thing that matters / B. think comprehensively / C. think in relative manner / D. patient's condition and diagnosis can change at any moment)
A — "tongue diagnosis is the only thing that matters" is the incorrect statement. TCM diagnosis is explicitly holistic, using four examination methods (looking, listening/smelling, asking, palpating), not any single method in isolation.
✓ verified against course material

01Discuss the meaning behind the title 'The Sack of Pearls' by Zhang Yuan Su and explain why he may have chosen this title for his book.
"Zhen Zhu Nang" (珍珠囊, "Pearl Sack/Bag of Pearls") frames the book's contents — condensed, essential knowledge about herb properties and formulas — as precious pearls gathered into a pouch: small in volume but high in concentrated value. The title signals that this is a distilled, practical reference rather than an exhaustive theoretical treatise.
⚠ double-check against the chapter/recording
02Which herb saved Kangxi from malaria in 1693?
Cinchona bark (quinine source) — given to the Kangxi Emperor by French Jesuit missionaries, a striking example of a Western remedy entering the Chinese imperial court directly.
✓ verified against course material

04What might ancient people have called acupuncturists? Explain your reasoning.
Worth checking the exact term the book uses here — early Chinese healers overlapped conceptually with shamans/diviners (巫, wu), and the Zhou-dynasty official medical hierarchy had specific role titles (e.g., 疾醫, 瘍醫) — but don't assert one specific historical title for "acupuncturist" as confirmed without checking the chapter directly.

05Who said, "To treat illness without understanding the viscera and bowels anatomy is no different from a blind man walking in the dark"?

Malaria — a clean parallel between the Chinese herbal tradition (qinghao, source of artemisinin) and the Western/Peruvian tradition (cinchona bark, source of quinine) independently converging on treatments for the same disease.

It's genuinely a bridge case rather than belonging cleanly to either category.

02What does the term 'Kanpō' (Kampo) literally mean?
"Han method" — 漢方, literally "[the] Han [dynasty/China]'s method/prescriptions," referring to medicine derived from Chinese methods as adapted and practiced in Japan.
✓ verified against course material
03Who was the Korean acupuncturist in the late 16th century who taught acupuncture to the Japanese and influenced the book 'Shinkyu Gokuhi Sho'?
Kim Deuk Bang (also transliterated Kim Deokbang) — named directly in the course material.

04Why did the Japanese Kohoha school focus exclusively on studying 'Shang Han Lun'?
Yoshimasu Todo, founder of the Koho-ha school, rejected abstract yin-yang and Five Phase theorizing in favor of pragmatic, empirically testable clinical formulas. The Shang Han Lun's concrete, pattern-based clinical protocols fit that "back to the clinical classics" philosophy far better than the more theory-laden Nei Jing did.
