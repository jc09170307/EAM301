Why "Week by Week" Alone Isn't Enough

Week-by-week study is still correct for your first pass through new material — it matches how lectures unfold and how your Workbook, Worksheet, and Study Guide are organized, chapter by chapter. That part of your habit doesn't need to change.

What breaks is the assumption that finishing a chapter means you can set it down. With no midterm to "close out" the first half, both exams can pull from anything, any week, at any point in that one 4-day window. A chapter you haven't touched since Week 3 needs to be just as fresh in Week 10 as the chapter from Week 9 — that's the entire logic behind everything else in this guide.

🗡️
The highest-leverage material isn't the textbookChoi said it directly: essay credit goes to what was discussed in class — not the book, not AI. Every "Straight from Week X class" callout across your Workbook, Study Guide, and Cram Sheet is drawn from actual lecture transcripts and is worth more per minute spent than re-reading the textbook chapters. When your review time is limited, start there.

The Study Pathway — Three Passes Through Every Chapter

Not one read-through — three deliberately different passes, each using a different tool, spaced apart. This is what actually builds retention, versus re-reading the same page four times and feeling like you studied.

1
First pass — Understand. Read the Workbook chapter page, then the matching Study Guide narrative. Goal: understand the argument, not memorize facts yet. Do this the same week the lecture happens.
2
Second pass — Test yourself. A few days later, close the Workbook and open the Worksheet cold. Fill in what you remember before checking the answer key. Where you blank, that's your real weak spot — not what you assume it is.
3
Third pass — Drill recognition. Run that chapter's questions in the Quiz Bank and check the Question Bank for anything Choi flagged specifically. This is fast, low-effort, and catches facts the first two passes didn't stick.
🐉
Space these passes out — don't do all three in one sittingA few days between passes is what makes this spaced repetition instead of just re-reading three times in a row. Same content, wildly different retention.

Exam-Window Game Plan

The window opens Wednesday night and runs roughly four days. Both exams are open-book but strictly timed once started — so the game changes from "recall everything" to "know exactly where everything is."

1
Don't take either exam the moment the window opens. Sleep on it at least one night — you want the interleaved review from the days before still fresh, not rushed.
2
Open the Fast-Lookup Index in a second browser tab before you start either exam. You've already rehearsed with it — use it exactly the way you practiced.
3
Consider taking Test A first — lower weight (40%), so it functions as a warm-up and confidence check before the higher-stakes Test B (50%).
4
For essay questions, reach for the "Straight from class" content first — it's what scores highest, and it's exactly what the Cram Sheet's Full-Credit Phrases page and the Workbook's Week-specific callouts were built to surface fast.
5
Pace yourself against the real numbers Prof. Choi gave in Week 10: roughly 50 questions/hour. Test A runs ~40–43 questions total, with about 10 (under 25%) as open/essay — most of those are short answers, not long-form. Test B runs more open questions (~12–13), with the rest multiple choice. Budget your time knowing Test B will eat more clock per question.
✍️
Essay questions Choi gave as real examples — study these patterns, not just factsTest A: "Which cultural hero among the Three Sovereigns introduced medicine and agriculture?" (simple/factual) and "What does Wu Wei mean? Explain with a patient-care example involving terminal cancer" (needs synthesis + application). Test B: "Why did the Japanese Kohoha school study the Shanghan Lun?" and a reflection question on the most valuable lesson from the course. Notice the pattern — one factual-recall essay, one applied-synthesis essay, per test.
⚠️
Choi's own warning: don't lean on AI for the essaysHe said directly that AI-generated answers won't match what he taught — his framing (e.g. Wu Wei via terminal cancer care, not a textbook definition) is specific to the lecture, not to a generic search result. Draft essay answers from the transcripts and Cram Sheet phrasing, not from a chatbot cold.
🏆
You've already rehearsed this exact situationBy the time you reach the real window, you'll have run at least one full-length Mock Exam with the Fast-Lookup Index open beside it. The real exam should feel like a rehearsed motion, not a new experience — that's the entire point of building these tools before it counted.
